The Last Passenger Pigeon Had A Name, And A Date

PALEODEX · 1 September 2026 · 7 min read

A century before she died, her species may have been the most abundant bird in North America. Almost no extinct species gets a date of death. This one does — and the paperwork behind it is thinner and stranger than the date makes it look.

Extinction usually happens without a receipt. A species stops being recorded, then stops being looked for, and years later somebody works out that the last sighting was probably the last one. The date, if there is one at all, is a retrospective guess.

The passenger pigeon is the exception, and that is the interesting part of the story — not that it died out, but that we can put a day on it.

Wilson timed a flock and did the arithmetic

In 1810, near Frankfort, Kentucky, the ornithologist Alexander Wilson watched a single flock of passenger pigeons pass overhead. It took four hours, with stragglers for about an hour more. Wilson did what a naturalist of his period did with an observation like that: he estimated. Assuming a column roughly a mile wide moving at about sixty miles an hour, he worked out a length of some 240 miles, multiplied through by an assumed density, and arrived at a figure he printed in American Ornithology: 2,230,272,000 birds.

It is worth being precise about what that number is. It is a calculation, not a count — density times width times speed, with every term assumed. A. W. Schorger, whose 1955 monograph is still the source of record for this species, treated continental totals of three to five billion as probably generous, while allowing that Wilson had undoubtedly seen well over a billion birds in one sitting. Schorger also reckoned the species accounted for somewhere between a quarter and two fifths of every land bird in the United States.

So: the biggest number in the story is an estimate, and the people who study it say so. Hold that thought, because it comes back.

The telegraph found them, the railway moved them

What happened next was not a slow decline. It was an industry, and it turned on two pieces of nineteenth-century infrastructure arriving at the same time.

Passenger pigeons nested communally, in enormous concentrations, in predictable places. Once the telegraph could report a nesting the moment it was found, and the railways could carry the result to city markets before it spoiled, a nesting site stopped being a local event and became a supply. Birds were netted at the roost and packed into barrels with ice or salt.

The scale is in shipping records rather than in anyone's estimate. Eight shippers in Grand Rapids, Michigan sent out 688 barrels — 105,855 pounds of pigeon — between 28 March and 11 July 1860. At the great Petoskey nesting of 1878, professional hunters worked for months at an estimated 50,000 birds a day: roughly 1.5 million dead, plus another 80,352 shipped out alive for trap shooting.

By 1914 there was one left, in a cage at the Cincinnati Zoological Garden.

Her name was Martha, and her paperwork disagrees with itself

She was called Martha. She was photographed, more than once, while she was alive — which is itself unusual for an animal that was about to become the last of its kind.

Almost everything else about her is softer than you would expect. The Cincinnati Zoo's administration building and its written records burned in 1963, so there is no ledger to check. Her age at death is an estimate, not a record: published figures run from seventeen to twenty-nine years, and twenty-nine is a convention repeated so often that it reads like data. A Smithsonian photograph card, typed in 1946, states flatly that she was "hatched in the Cincinnati Zoo in 1888" — which does not square with the 1885 that produces the familiar twenty-nine.

The accounts of the day she died disagree too. Robert W. Shufeldt, who dissected the bird and published on it in The Auk four months later, recorded a time of death on the authority of the zoo's superintendent, Sol Stephan. Later work — Errol Fuller's 2014 book on the species among it — records that Stephan and his son Joseph gave conflicting versions, one of them putting the death several hours later than the other, and that the accounts do not even agree on who was in the room. Some say a group of keepers; some say the two Stephans; one has her found alone by an assistant keeper.

We have deliberately left the hour out of this article, and out of the video that goes with it. Two contemporary accounts disagree and nothing survives to settle them. What they do agree on is the afternoon, and the day.

1 September 1914

That is the date, and it survived everything else. It was in the scientific literature within four months. It is cast in bronze on the Langdon Club memorial at the zoo, on the building where she lived.

The same plaque carries a second name and a second date: Incas, the last known Carolina parakeet, 21 February 1918, in the same building. It is a useful correction to have standing right there in the metal, because it stops the obvious overstatement. Martha is not the only extinct animal with a recorded date of death. She is one of a very small number — and for almost every other extinct species, there is no date at all.

What the story is actually about

It would be easy to tell this as a fable about a bird going from billions to one. That version is true and it is also the least interesting reading available, because it flattens the thing that makes the case unusual.

What is unusual is the documentation, and how uneven it is. The population figure that opens the story is an estimate built on assumptions, published by a man who said as much. The collapse is recorded in commercial shipping weights, which are about as hard as historical numbers get. The last individual's age is a guess, her hour of death is disputed between two people who were supposedly present, and her zoo's records burned half a century later. And yet the date came through all of that intact, because it happened recently enough, publicly enough, and in a country that printed newspapers and owned cameras.

Extinction is normally something we notice afterwards. This one was watched.
